# What not to tell a mortgage lender, and what you must share

Written by Samantha Shelton NMLS 1647301 · Updated November 16, 2026

Applying for a Michigan mortgage? There is nothing material to hide. Lenders verify income, assets and debts, including support set through a Friend of the Court order, and a knowingly false statement can be a federal crime. You need not discuss what lenders cannot consider, such as plans to have children. The bigger risk is what you do before closing.

**Key takeaways**

- Nothing material to hide: knowingly false statements to a mortgage lending business fall under 18 U.S.C. §1014, and underwriting checks credit reports, tax records, bank statements and employment anyway.
- The Equal Credit Opportunity Act bars lenders from treating you differently because of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age or public assistance income, and Regulation B bars questions about plans to have children.
- Eight moves to avoid before closing, from new credit and a financed car to undocumented cash deposits and co-signing, are in the table below.
- Explain unusual items early. Fannie Mae treats a single deposit over 50% of your monthly qualifying income as a large deposit, and a short letter of explanation with the paper trail is the usual fix.

## Is there anything you should not tell a mortgage lender?

Nothing material should be hidden. The application asks about your income, debts, assets, the property and legal matters such as judgments or bankruptcy, and the lender checks each one. You are not required to discuss things the law bars lenders from considering, and you should not offer guesses about your future as if they were facts.

The honest version of "what not to tell" is short. You do not need to bring up a pregnancy, your religion or plans to have children, because a lender may not use them. And keep hopes separate from facts: a raise that is not yet in writing, or a bonus your employer has not confirmed, is something to mention as a possibility, not to list as income. Everything the application asks, answer accurately.

## What can't a mortgage lender ask you or use against you?

Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, a lender cannot treat you less favorably because of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age (if you are old enough to sign a contract), public assistance income, or exercising your consumer credit rights. It also cannot ask about plans to have children. The demographic questions on the application are optional.

The [Equal Credit Opportunity Act](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/) is carried out through the CFPB's Regulation B. Its [rules on requests for information](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/5/) let a lender ask how many dependents you have, their ages and what they cost you, because those affect your budget. A lender may not ask about birth control or whether you plan to have or raise children, as the CFPB explains in [its answer on children and dependents](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/i-want-to-apply-for-a-mortgage-or-home-equity-loan-can-a-lender-or-broker-ask-me-about-my-children-or-dependents-en-355/). [VERIFY: 12 CFR 1002.5(d) paragraph numbers after the CFPB's 2026 Regulation B amendments]

A few questions look personal but are allowed. The application asks whether you are married, unmarried or separated, but your answer cannot be used against you. [VERIFY: 12 CFR 1002.5(d)(1)] If you receive alimony, child support or separate maintenance, you do not have to reveal that income unless you want it counted. The [Uniform Residential Loan Application](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/delivering/uniform-mortgage-data-program/uniform-residential-loan-application) says so in its income section. The demographic section asks about ethnicity, race and sex so regulators can monitor fair lending, and answering is your choice. [VERIFY: URLA income-section note and Section 8; 12 CFR 1002.13]

This is a plain-English summary of federal rules, not legal advice. Align Lending is an Equal Housing Opportunity company.

## What do you have to tell a mortgage lender?

Tell the lender every source of income you want counted, all debts and monthly obligations including support you pay, your assets and where your down payment comes from, including gifts, any other real estate you own, and legal matters the application asks about, such as bankruptcy, foreclosure, judgments or lawsuits.

The application's declarations section asks many of these as yes-or-no questions, including whether you co-signed a debt you have not listed and whether you are applying for new credit before closing. [VERIFY: URLA Section 5a and 5b wording] Michigan files add a few items that national checklists leave out.

| What to share | Why lenders ask | How it is usually verified |
|---|---|---|
| Income you want counted | It sets the payment you can qualify for | Pay stubs, W-2s or tax returns, IRS transcripts and a call to your employer |
| Every debt and monthly obligation | Debts set your debt-to-income ratio (DTI), your monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income | Your credit report, plus statements for debts that do not report |
| Child support or alimony you pay, including a Michigan Friend of the Court order | Fannie Mae counts court-ordered payments with more than 10 months left as a monthly debt | The court order or judgment, plus payment history from MiChildSupport |
| Gifts toward the purchase | The money has to be a gift, not a loan you will repay | A signed gift letter and proof of the transfer |
| Other homes you own, including a Michigan home with a Principal Residence Exemption (PRE) | Their payments count, and Michigan allows one PRE at a time, so your occupancy answer has to match | Mortgage statements, tax bills and insurance |
| Land contract payments you make | A monthly payment to a seller is a debt like any other | The contract and proof of each payment |
| Tax liens and delinquent property taxes, including a Michigan Treasury lien | Unpaid taxes and liens can come ahead of a new mortgage | Title search, county treasurer records and the credit report |
| A past bankruptcy, foreclosure or short sale | The application asks about the last 7 years | Credit report and court or public records |
| A job change you have accepted or are planning | Income has to be current and likely to continue | Employment is verified again before closing |

Sources for the table: Fannie Mae Selling Guide [B3-6-05, Monthly Debt Obligations](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-05/monthly-debt-obligations); the State of Michigan's [MiChildSupport](https://www.michigan.gov/michildsupport) portal; Fannie Mae [B3-4.3-04, Personal Gifts](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-04/personal-gifts); Michigan Treasury on the [Principal Residence Exemption](https://www.michigan.gov/taxes/property/principal-residence-exemption). [VERIFY: each Michigan row as an underwriting item against the current URLA and Selling Guide]

Two Michigan details are worth knowing. Unpaid Michigan property taxes are returned to the county treasurer as delinquent each March 1, as Michigan Treasury's [foreclosure process page](https://www.michigan.gov/taxes/property/forfeiture-foreclosure/county/foreclosure-process) explains, so a title search will find them. And if you pay a seller on a land contract, the lender will want that payment history; see [how a Michigan land contract works](/learn/michigan-land-contract/). Gift rules are covered in [financing a home purchase with gift funds](/learn/financing-home-purchase-with-gift-funds/), and lenders trace the money behind [earnest money deposits](/learn/earnest-money-deposits/) the same way.

## What happens if you leave something out?

Most omissions surface in underwriting through the credit report, IRS tax transcripts, bank statements or a call to your employer, and a late discovery can delay or end the approval. A knowingly false statement on a loan application can be a federal crime. Telling your loan officer early gives them time to find a program that fits.

The statute is [18 U.S.C. §1014](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/1014), which covers knowingly false statements made to influence a mortgage lending business, among other institutions. It is aimed at deliberate falsehoods. If you notice an honest mistake on your application, tell your loan officer so it can be corrected.

Disclosure also does practical work. Loan programs treat the same item differently, so a loan officer who knows about a collection account or a past short sale on the first call can compare the programs that allow it. Found the week before closing, the same item leaves fewer choices.

## What should you not do after applying for a mortgage?

Until closing, do not open new credit, finance a car or furniture, change jobs without telling your loan officer, move large sums without a paper trail, deposit cash you cannot document, co-sign for anyone or miss a payment. Each one can change the approval, because the approval rests on the file the lender verified.

| Don't | Why it matters | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Open a credit card or loan | New debt can change your DTI and your credit, and the application asks about new credit before closing | Wait until after closing, or ask your loan officer first |
| Finance a car, furniture or appliances | A new monthly payment counts against your income | Shop now and buy after closing |
| Change jobs or start self-employment without telling your loan officer | Lenders verify employment again close to closing | Call your loan officer before you accept an offer |
| Move large sums between accounts without records | Lenders may ask where large deposits came from | Keep every statement and transfer record |
| Deposit cash you cannot document | Money with no paper trail may not count toward your closing funds | Ask your loan officer before you deposit it |
| Co-sign for someone | Their debt counts as yours | Wait until after closing |
| Miss or pay late on any bill | A new late payment can lower your credit score | Turn on autopay for every account |
| Spend the savings you showed the lender | The approval assumed those funds would be there | Keep balances steady until closing |

If debt is the tight spot, read [how to improve your debt-to-income ratio](/learn/how-to-improve-your-debt-to-income-ratio/) before you apply, not after.

## What should you do if something changes before closing?

Tell your loan officer right away about a new job, a raise, a lost job, a new debt, a large deposit or a change in who will live in the home. Many changes are workable when the lender learns early. Some need updated documents, and some lead to a revised disclosure of your loan terms and costs.

A revised Loan Estimate can follow certain changes under federal disclosure rules. [VERIFY: 12 CFR 1026.19(e)(3)(iv) changed circumstances] A planned leave is one example of a change worth raising: Fannie Mae has [temporary leave income rules](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.3-09/temporary-leave-income) for leave such as parental or medical leave, and the lender can apply them only if it knows. The [home closing process](/learn/home-closing-process/) guide shows where these checks fall in the timeline.

## How do you explain a late payment, gap in work or large deposit?

Underwriters usually ask for a short, factual letter of explanation. It should say what happened and when, why it will not continue, and what proof is attached, such as a medical bill, a layoff notice or the statement that shows where a deposit came from. Keep it to the facts, and keep it short.

For deposits, Fannie Mae defines a large deposit as a single deposit over 50% of your total monthly qualifying income, and the lender may ask for its source under [Selling Guide B3-4.2-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.2-02/depository-accounts). Align's underwriting guide has a [letter of explanation, with a sample](/learn/mortgage-underwriting/).

## What should you tell a mortgage broker on the first call?

Share your goal, whether that is buying, refinancing or using equity, plus your rough income and debts, anything unusual in your credit or work history, how much cash you have and where it sits, and your timeline. A first conversation does not require a credit pull, and it lets the broker point you to programs that fit.

Rough numbers are fine at this stage. The documents come later, when you apply, and that is when each line is checked. If your credit needs work first, start with [credit repair steps before you apply](/learn/credit-repair-strategies-for-home-buyers/), and read [why preapproval matters](/learn/importance-of-mortgage-preapproval/) before you shop for homes.

## Questions about what to tell a mortgage lender

### What should you not tell a mortgage lender?

Nothing false, and nothing you present as fact when it is a guess. Answer every question on the application accurately, including debts, other homes and past credit events. You do not need to raise things lenders cannot consider, such as your religion or plans to have children, and you need not reveal alimony or child support you receive unless you want it counted.

### What not to say to a mortgage broker?

Avoid saying anything you cannot back up later, such as a raise not yet in writing or cash savings with no paper trail. A broker works from what you share, so leaving out a debt or a past late payment only moves the surprise to underwriting. Say what you know, flag what you are unsure about, and let the broker ask follow-up questions.

### Do you have to tell a lender you are pregnant or plan to have children?

No. Regulation B bars lenders from asking about plans to have or raise children, or assuming your income will fall because you might have them. A planned leave that will change your pay is different, because income is part of the application. Telling your loan officer about the leave lets the lender apply Fannie Mae's temporary leave rules instead of finding the change later.
<!-- VERIFY: 12 CFR 1002.5(d) and 1002.6(b)(3); Fannie Mae B3-3.3-09 -->

### Do lenders check bank statements for cash deposits?

Yes. Lenders review recent bank statements, and Fannie Mae treats a single deposit over 50% of your total monthly qualifying income as a large deposit that may need a documented source. Cash with no paper trail may not count toward your down payment or closing costs. If you have cash at home, ask your loan officer before you deposit it.
<!-- VERIFY: Fannie Mae B3-4.2-02 current text on purchase transactions and unsourced funds -->

### Can you change jobs while getting a mortgage?

Sometimes, but tell your loan officer before you accept the offer. Lenders verify employment again shortly before closing, and a move in the same line of work at similar or higher pay is often easier to document than a switch to commission, hourly or self-employed income. Your loan officer can tell you what the new job would require.
<!-- VERIFY: Fannie Mae employment history and verbal verification of employment sections -->

### Is it illegal to lie on a mortgage application?

Yes. Under 18 U.S.C. §1014, knowingly making a false statement to influence a mortgage lending business is a federal crime, punishable by a fine of up to $1,000,000, up to 30 years in prison, or both. Honest mistakes are different: if you notice an error on your application, tell your loan officer so it can be corrected.
<!-- VERIFY: 18 U.S.C. §1014 penalty text at govinfo.gov -->

### Do I have to tell my lender about a side business?

You do not have to use side-business income to qualify. The business will still appear on your tax returns, though, and any debt, tax balance or lien tied to it belongs on the application like any other obligation. Tell your loan officer about it, so the lender can decide how the income or losses are treated.
<!-- VERIFY: Fannie Mae B3-3.5-01 treatment of secondary self-employment not used to qualify -->
