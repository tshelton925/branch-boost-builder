# Can you get a mortgage at 60, 70 or 80? How lenders treat retirement income

Written by Samantha Shelton NMLS 1647301 · Updated November 16, 2026

<div class="al-answer">

Yes. Federal law bars lenders from turning you down or cutting your loan term because of your age, so at 60, 70 or 80 you can take a 30-year mortgage if your income and credit qualify. Lenders count Social Security, pensions, retirement withdrawals and, in some programs, savings. Michigan movers should budget for the property tax reset after a purchase.

</div>

<div class="al-takeaways">

**Key takeaways**

- The Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B bar age discrimination in credit, and a lender cannot discount income because it is a pension, annuity or other retirement benefit.
- Social Security, pensions, annuities and retirement account withdrawals all count. Fannie Mae asks that retirement account withdrawals continue for at least 3 years from the note date.
- The loan term is your choice at any age. The same terms are open at 62 or 82 as at 32.
- In Michigan, a home's taxable value jumped a typical 50.5% the year after it sold in 2026, so a smaller home can carry a larger tax bill.

</div>

## Can a lender deny you a mortgage because of your age?

No. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act and its rule, Regulation B, bar lenders from discriminating because of age, so a lender cannot turn you down or limit your loan term because you are older. Age can be considered only in narrow, permitted ways. The lender still checks your income, credit and the property, as it would for anyone.

Regulation B allows a few narrow uses of age. A credit scoring system that has been statistically tested may use age as a factor, but it cannot give an applicant 62 or older a lower score because of age. Any system may also use age in a way that favors an applicant 62 or older. In a judgment-based review, age may be weighed only as it bears on another real element of creditworthiness. `[VERIFY: 12 CFR 1002.6(b)(2)(ii) and (iv), 1002.2(o) and comment 6(b)(2)-3, CFPB]`

The rule also protects retirement income. A lender may not discount or exclude income because it is a pension, an annuity or another retirement benefit. It may still weigh how much you receive and how long it will last. `[VERIFY: 12 CFR 1002.6(b)(5)]` You can read the rule in the [CFPB's text of Regulation B, section 1002.6](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/6/).

If you think a lender treated you differently because of your age, you can [submit a complaint to the CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/).

## Can you get a 30-year mortgage at 70 or 75?

Yes. The loan term is your choice, not a function of your age. Some older borrowers choose a shorter term to finish paying sooner. Others prefer the lower required payment of a longer term and plan to pay extra or sell later. Both are allowed, and the lender qualifies you on the same income and credit rules.

No rule ties the length of a mortgage to life expectancy. A mortgage also does not disappear when a borrower dies. It is paid from the estate or from the sale of the home, and federal rules let certain heirs keep the home and take over the loan. `[VERIFY: 12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d); 12 CFR 1024.31 successor in interest]`

What decides the term is your plan. A shorter term usually means a larger required payment and less interest over the life of the loan. A longer term keeps the required payment smaller and leaves more cash free each month. The guide to the [15-year vs. 30-year mortgage](/learn/15-year-vs-30-year-mortgage-comparison/) compares the two. `[COMPLIANCE REVIEW: D7 term names]`

Your plan for your current home matters as much as the term. If you will sell one home and buy another, the order of the two closings shapes the loan, so [plan a buy-before-you-sell move](/purchase/buy-before-you-sell/) before you make an offer.

## How do lenders count Social Security and retirement income?

Lenders count Social Security retirement, pensions and annuities when you document them with an award letter, statements or tax returns. Withdrawals from retirement accounts count if they can continue for at least three years. Non-taxable income, such as part of your Social Security, may be grossed up, meaning increased for qualifying under each program's rules.

Grossing up exists because lenders compare your debts with gross, pre-tax income. Income you pay no tax on goes further, so Fannie Mae and HUD each let lenders adjust it upward by a set method. `[VERIFY: Fannie Mae B3-3.1-01; HUD 4000.1 II.A.4 gross-up methods]` Your tax return shows how much of your Social Security is taxable.

| Income type | What lenders usually ask for | Continuance or special rule |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security retirement | Award letter or benefit statement, or recent tax returns, plus proof you receive it | Benefits on your own work record need no extra proof that they will continue; benefits drawn on another person's record must be shown to continue 3 years |
| Social Security disability (SSDI) | Award letter or tax returns, plus proof of receipt | Treated like retirement benefits on your own record |
| Pension | Award letter or pension statement, plus proof of receipt | Lender checks for an end date or a scheduled reduction |
| Annuity | Annuity contract or statement, plus proof of receipt | Must continue at least 3 years from the note date |
| IRA or 401(k) withdrawals | Account statements showing regular withdrawals and the balance | Must continue at least 3 years from the note date |
| Interest and dividends | Tax returns or account statements | Usually averaged; the assets must stay in place to keep paying |
| Rental income | Leases and tax returns | Program rules on how much of the rent counts |

Sources: Fannie Mae Selling Guide sections B3-3.4-03, B3-3.4-15 and B3-3.1-01; HUD Handbook 4000.1 for FHA loans. Lenders can add stricter requirements, called overlays. `[VERIFY each row against Fannie Mae B3-3.4-03, B3-3.4-08, B3-3.4-15 and HUD 4000.1]`

Supplemental Security Income and disability income have their own rules, covered in the guide to buying a home on SSI or disability income. `[LINK WHEN LIVE: /learn/buying-a-home-on-ssi-or-disability-income/]` To see how your figures turn into a price range, try the [home affordability calculator](/home-affordability-calculator/).

## Can you qualify for a mortgage using your savings instead of income?

Sometimes. Fannie Mae lets lenders count income from certain employment-related assets, such as retirement accounts, under set rules. Non-QM asset depletion loans, which fall outside the standard agency rules, divide eligible assets over a set period to create qualifying income. Terms, costs and requirements on these loans differ from standard loans.

Under [Fannie Mae's employment-related assets rule](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.4-06/employment-related-assets-qualifying-income), the lender divides your net documented assets by the loan's term in months to arrive at a monthly income figure. Only assets that came from work qualify, such as a 401(k), an IRA or a severance payout. Money from an inheritance, a lawsuit or the sale of real estate does not. Funds you need to close are not counted as income. `[VERIFY: Fannie Mae B3-3.4-06 eligibility, including loan-to-value and transaction limits]`

Non-qualified mortgage (non-QM) loans are not sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. Each lender sets its own method, including which accounts count and how much of each balance it uses. Align's page on [asset depletion loans in Michigan](/product-loans/asset-based-income-loans/) explains how that path works.

## What are the mortgage options for people over 60?

People over 60 have the same options as any borrower: conventional loans, FHA loans, VA loans for eligible veterans and USDA loans in eligible areas. Some also buy with cash, use a bridge loan or a buy-before-you-sell plan, or, as a separate product, take a reverse mortgage, which is for homeowners 62 and older.

- **Conventional loans** follow Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac rules. For Fannie Mae loans, its Desktop Underwriter evaluates credit, and lenders may add overlays.
- **FHA loans** are insured by HUD and made by FHA-approved lenders. They leave more room on credit history, in exchange for mortgage insurance that usually lasts longer.
- **VA loans** are for eligible veterans, service members and some surviving spouses.
- **USDA loans** are for homes in eligible rural areas, with household income limits.
- **Cash or a bridge loan.** Some sellers buy the next home first and repay short-term financing from the sale. The guide to [bridge loans](/learn/bridge-loans/) covers how they are repaid.

A reverse mortgage (HECM) is a different product with HUD-required counseling; see [HUD's HECM page](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/sfh/hecm/hecmabou). `[VERIFY: HUD HECM age rule, 62 and older]` `[DECISION D1 PENDING: link to /learn/reverse-mortgages-how-they-work/ only if Align confirms it brokers reverse mortgages]`

If you are shopping for features such as a step-free entry or a first-floor bedroom, the guide to [buying a home for aging in place](/learn/buying-home-for-aging-in-place/) covers what to check.

## What happens to your property taxes when you downsize in Michigan?

Buying a new Michigan home resets its taxable value the next year to the state equalized value, half of the assessor's market value, not automatically half the price you paid. Because your old home's taxable value was capped for years, a smaller home can cost as much or more in property tax.

While one owner keeps a home, [Michigan law](https://legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-211-27a) caps the yearly rise in taxable value at inflation or 5%, whichever is lower. A sale ends the cap. Align's [2026 Michigan Property Tax Uncapping Study](/research/michigan-property-tax-uncapping-study/) puts the typical statewide jump in taxable value the year after a sale at about 50.5%. The guide to [how Michigan taxable value resets after a sale](/learn/michigan-taxable-value/) explains each value on a tax record.

**Hypothetical example, not an offer. Actual rates, costs and payments vary.**

You sell a home you have owned for decades, whose taxable value had been capped at about $90,000, and you buy a $260,000 condo. Assume the assessor's market value matches the price. The condo's state equalized value, and so its taxable value after the reset, is then about $130,000. Both bills use the statewide median homestead rate of 36.0 mills (2025 rates).

| Property tax only | Old home | New condo, after the reset |
|---|---|---|
| Taxable value | about $90,000 | about $130,000 |
| Millage (statewide median homestead, 2025 rates) | 36.0 mills | 36.0 mills |
| Annual property tax | about $3,240 | about $4,680 |

Annual tax = taxable value ÷ 1,000 × millage. Your city or township, school district and county set your actual millage, and the homestead rate assumes you file the Principal Residence Exemption. Source: Align's 2026 Uncapping Study. The example shows taxes only, no loan figures.

Before you make an offer, [estimate your Michigan property tax after you buy](/michigan-property-tax-calculator/) with the real address.

## What Michigan property tax help is there for seniors?

There is no automatic senior property tax cut in Michigan. Older homeowners may qualify for the homestead property tax credit on the state income tax return, a deferment that lets some homeowners 62 and older pay summer taxes later, and, with low income, a poverty exemption granted by the local board of review.

- **Homestead property tax credit.** You claim it on Form MI-1040CR if your household resources and your home's taxable value fall under the state's limits, and claimants 65 or older can receive a larger share. See [Treasury's homestead property tax credit page](https://www.michigan.gov/taxes/iit/tax-guidance/credits-exemptions/hptc) and Align's guide to the Michigan homestead property tax credit. `[LINK WHEN LIVE: /learn/michigan-homestead-property-tax-credit/]` `[VERIFY: senior share and current limits, MI-1040CR instructions]`
- **Summer tax deferment.** Homeowners 62 or older, and some other groups, with total household income of $40,000 or less for the prior year can ask their city or township treasurer to defer the summer bill. Payment is then due by February 14 without penalty or interest. The rule is in [MCL 211.51](https://legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-211-51). `[VERIFY: income limit, September 14 or 15 filing deadline and February 14 date, Treasury]`
- **Poverty exemption.** Any homeowner with low income can apply to the local board of review on Forms 5737 and 5739. The board can reduce taxable value in full or in part, under income guidelines set by each city or township. See [Treasury's poverty exemption fact sheet](https://www.michigan.gov/documents/treasury/Poverty_Exemption_Taxpayer_Fact_Sheet_728331_7.pdf). `[VERIFY: MCL 211.7u, one-year term]`

These programs are separate from [Michigan's Principal Residence Exemption](/learn/homestead-exemption-benefits-and-filing-guide/), which exempts a home you live in from up to 18 mills of school operating tax. The seller's exemption does not carry over, so file your own by June 1 or November 1.

## Should you pay off your mortgage before you retire?

It depends on your income, savings, taxes and the interest rate on your loan. Paying it off removes a monthly payment, while keeping it can leave savings available for health care, repairs or other needs. There is no single right answer, so compare your own numbers and talk with a tax or financial professional.

The trade-offs usually come down to three questions:

- **Where would the payoff money come from?** A large withdrawal from a pre-tax retirement account can raise your taxable income for that year.
- **How much cash do you want on hand?** Money paid into a home is harder to reach later. Borrowing against it again means qualifying again.
- **What stays the same either way?** Property taxes and homeowners insurance continue after a payoff. You pay them directly instead of through escrow.

## Questions about mortgages for older homebuyers

### Can a 75-year-old get a 30-year mortgage?

Yes. Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B, a lender cannot refuse you or offer only a shorter term because you are 75. You still need to qualify on income, credit and the property, like any borrower. Whether a 30-year or shorter term suits you depends on your plans and budget.

### Is 55 too old to buy a house?

No. There is no upper age limit for buying a home or getting a mortgage, and lenders may not use your age against you. What matters is whether your income, savings and credit support the loan, and whether the home fits your plans for the years ahead, including any accessibility features you want.

### Can you buy a house on Social Security alone?

Yes, if the benefit covers the payment and your other debts within the lender's limits. Social Security retirement and disability income count as qualifying income with an award letter or tax records, and the non-taxable portion may be grossed up under program rules. Savings, a co-borrower or a smaller loan can also help.

### What mortgage options do people over 70 have?

The right option depends on your income, savings, equity and plans. People over 70 can use conventional, FHA, VA (if eligible) and USDA (in eligible areas) loans, buy with cash, or use a bridge loan when moving between homes. Homeowners 62 and older can also look at HUD's reverse mortgage program, which is a separate product.

### Do lenders count retirement account withdrawals as income?

Yes, under set rules. Fannie Mae counts withdrawals from accounts such as an IRA or 401(k) when you document them and show they can continue for at least three years from the note date. Fannie Mae's employment-related assets rule can also turn eligible retirement savings into qualifying income without regular withdrawals.

### Do property taxes go down when you turn 65 in Michigan?

No. Michigan has no automatic property tax reduction at 65. Older homeowners may qualify for a larger share of the homestead property tax credit, a summer tax deferment from age 62 with a household income limit, and, with low income, a poverty exemption from the local board of review. Each one requires a claim or an application.

### Is it smart to pay off your house when you retire?

It depends on your situation. Paying off the loan removes a payment, but it can use savings you may need later, and withdrawals from pre-tax accounts can raise your taxable income. Keeping the loan keeps cash available. Compare your own numbers with a tax or financial professional; this is general education, not advice.
