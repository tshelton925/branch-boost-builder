# What income do you need to buy a house in Michigan?

Written by Samantha Shelton NMLS 1647301
Updated November 16, 2026

For Michigan homebuyers, there is no single salary you need. In 2025 the median buyer income on Michigan purchase loans was $91,000, and the median home value was $285,000 (Align's HMDA report). Lenders cap housing costs at a share of gross income. In Michigan, the new owner's property tax, not the seller's, takes a large part of that share.

**Key takeaways**

- In 2025 the median buyer income on Michigan purchase loans was $91,000 and the median home value was $285,000, about 3.1 times income (2025 HMDA data).
- At a 28% housing ratio, each $100 of monthly housing cost needs about $4,300 of yearly gross income.
- After a sale resets taxable value, Michigan's 2025 median homestead rate of 36.0 mills works out to about 1.8% of market value a year.
- This guide stops at income and tax. Run principal and interest in the affordability calculator with your own quoted rate.

This guide works from your income. For the number that still feels comfortable once real life is counted, read [How much house can you comfortably afford in Michigan?](/purchase/budget-review/)

## How much do Michigan homebuyers earn?

In 2025 the median buyer with a Michigan purchase loan reported $91,000 of income, and the median home value on those loans was $285,000, about 3.1 times income. Across the 10 counties with the most loans, median values ran from $215,000 in Ingham to $435,000 in Washtenaw (Align Lending, Michigan Mortgage Market Report, 2025 HMDA).

The figures come from [Align's Michigan Mortgage Market Report](/research/michigan-mortgage-market-report/), built from every 2025 application lenders reported in the federal HMDA data. The two medians come from the same loans but not from the same buyer, so the ratio describes the market, not any one household. HMDA records the value the lender relied on, usually the lower of the appraisal and the price, rounded to $10,000 bands.

## How do lenders decide how much income you need?

Lenders divide your monthly debts, including the new housing payment, by your gross monthly income. That is your debt-to-income ratio (DTI). The old 28/36 rule of thumb is a starting point, and automated underwriting often allows more. In 2025, 30% of Michigan purchase loans were made with a DTI above 43% (Align's HMDA report).

Two ratios matter. The housing ratio counts the new housing payment alone: principal, interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, any mortgage insurance and any association dues. The total ratio adds car loans, student loans, minimum card payments and other monthly debts. Rent you pay today does not count, because the new housing cost replaces it. The CFPB's explainer, [What is a debt-to-income ratio?](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-en-1791/), notes that loan products and lenders set different limits.

The rule of thumb keeps housing at 28% of gross income and all debts at 36%. The agencies' own limits differ:

- **Conventional (Fannie Mae).** The [Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-6-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-02/debt-income-ratios), caps total DTI at 36% on a manually underwritten loan, or up to 45% with qualifying credit and reserves. When Desktop Underwriter approves the file, the cap is 50%. [VERIFY: current B3-6-02 text; host blocked from this session]
- **Conventional (Freddie Mac).** Loan Product Advisor weighs the ratio file by file. The [Freddie Mac Guide, section 5401.2](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/section/5401.2), caps manually underwritten loans at 45%. [VERIFY: current 5401.2 text; host blocked from this session]
- **FHA.** [HUD Handbook 4000.1](https://www.hud.gov/hud-partners/single-family-handbook-4000-1) sets manual-underwriting benchmarks of 31% for housing and 43% for total debt for borrowers with credit scores of 580 or higher. Higher ratios are allowed when the file shows compensating factors, and HUD's TOTAL Mortgage Scorecard can approve higher ratios on its own. [VERIFY: current 4000.1 ratio matrix; host blocked from this session]

Lenders can also add stricter limits of their own, called overlays. In Align's report, 43% was a common quoted limit, yet 30% of 2025 Michigan purchase loans were above it, most of them between 44% and 49%. Denials clustered higher: 40.8% of denied purchase applications had a ratio of 50% or more, against 5.8% of the loans made. For each program's rules, see [debt-to-income limits by loan type](/learn/debt-to-income-ratio-dti/).

## How much of your budget does Michigan property tax take?

The year after a home sells, its taxable value resets to the state equalized value, half of the assessor's market value, not automatically half the price you paid. At Michigan's 2025 median homestead rate of 36.0 mills, that is about 1.8% of market value a year: about $5,400 on a $300,000 home, or $450 a month.

The reset comes from [MCL 211.27a](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-211-27a), which caps taxable value while one owner holds a home and lifts the cap after a transfer. In 2026 the reset raised a typical Michigan home's taxable value about 50.5% ([Align Lending's 2026 uncapping study](/research/michigan-property-tax-uncapping-study/)), so the listing's tax history understates the buyer's bill.

Hypothetical example, not an offer. Actual rates, costs and payments vary. On a $300,000 home at 36.0 mills, $450 a month of property tax alone uses about $19,300 of yearly gross income at a 28% housing ratio ($450 × 12 ÷ 0.28). That is income spoken for before insurance or the loan.

The address sets the rate. Millage comes from the city or township, the school district and the county, so two homes at the same price can carry different bills. County medians in the table run from 25.7 mills in Livingston to 53.9 in Ingham. To [estimate your Michigan property tax after you buy](/michigan-property-tax-calculator/), use the published millage for the address. The guide to [how Michigan taxable value resets after a sale](/learn/michigan-taxable-value/) shows the full calculation.

The 36.0-mill figure is a homestead rate. It assumes you file the [Principal Residence Exemption](https://www.michigan.gov/taxes/property/principal-residence-exemption), which exempts the home you live in from up to 18 mills of school operating tax. The seller's exemption does not carry over. File by June 1 for the summer levy, or by November 1 for the winter levy.

## Can I afford a $300,000 house on a $70,000 salary in Michigan?

Maybe. At a 28% housing ratio, a $70,000 income allows about $1,633 a month for all housing costs. A $300,000 Michigan home at the 36.0-mill median uses about $450 of that for property tax. Insurance, principal, interest and any mortgage insurance must fit in the rest, which depends on your rate, down payment and other debts.

Hypothetical example, not an offer. Actual rates, costs and payments vary. Here is the method, step by step.

1. Multiply your gross yearly income by 28% and divide by 12. For $70,000 that is about $1,633 a month.
2. Subtract the property tax for the address, not the listing's tax history. At 36.0 mills, a $300,000 home is about $450 a month, which leaves about $1,183.
3. Subtract homeowners insurance. Get a quote for the address from an insurance agent, because premiums vary with the home and the coverage.
4. Put what is left into the [home affordability calculator](/home-affordability-calculator/) with your quoted rate, your savings and your other debts. It works backward from income to a home price at your DTI limit.

> **Tool button:** [Estimate my property tax](/michigan-property-tax-calculator/)

Other debts shrink the room. At $70,000 with no other debts, the 28% housing ratio is the tighter limit. Once monthly debts pass about $467, the 36% total ratio becomes the tighter one ($2,100 − $1,633). If the rest cannot carry the loan at your quote, the same income may fit a lower price, a lower-millage address or a plan to pay down a debt first.

## What salary do you need for a $200,000, $400,000 or $500,000 home in Michigan?

Work from the price down. Michigan property tax scales with price: at 36.0 mills, about $3,600 a year on a $200,000 home, $7,200 on $400,000 and $9,000 on $500,000. The loan part depends on your rate, down payment and debts, so only a calculator run at your own quote gives the full income answer.

The address's own millage moves that figure. At 53.9 mills, the highest county median in the first table, a $400,000 home carries about $10,780 a year. At 25.7 mills, the lowest, it carries about $5,140.

## Does a lower income mean you can't buy in Michigan?

No. A lower income means a smaller housing budget, not a closed door. FHA loans, USDA loans in eligible rural areas and the MI Home Loan from the Michigan State Housing Development Authority (MSHDA) help buyers who have less cash. A lower-millage address also changes the math. The answer depends on your debts and the address.

FHA loans are insured by HUD and made by FHA-approved wholesale lenders. They leave more room on credit history, in exchange for mortgage insurance that usually lasts longer than on a conventional loan. USDA guaranteed loans apply only to eligible rural addresses, and USDA sets household income limits by area. [VERIFY: USDA Rural Development income eligibility; link USDA loan requirements, /learn/usda-loan-requirements/, once live]

MSHDA's MI Home Loan can be paired with MI 10K down payment assistance, and both have income and price limits. [VERIFY: current MSHDA program names and limits on michigan.gov/mshda at publish; link MSHDA MI Home Loan, /learn/mshda-mi-home-loan/, once live] MSHDA loans are made only through its participating lenders. Tax matters here too. Two homes at $250,000, one at 25.7 mills and one at 53.9 mills, differ by about $3,525 a year of tax. That is about $12,600 of yearly income at a 28% ratio. For more options, read the guides on [buying a home with low income](/learn/buying-home-with-low-income-tips-and-programs/) and [how to save for a down payment](/learn/how-to-save-for-down-payment/).

## What is the difference between what you qualify for and what you can afford?

Qualifying is a ratio a lender applies to your gross income. Affordability is whether the payment still works after childcare, savings, repairs and everything else your budget really holds. The second number is often lower, and it is the one to shop with. A lender sees the ratio. Only you see the rest of the month.

A lender works from gross pay. Your budget runs on take-home pay, after income tax, retirement savings and health insurance come out. It also carries costs no ratio counts, such as utilities, upkeep, commuting and a cushion for repairs. The guide to [budgeting for your first home](/learn/budgeting-for-your-first-home/) walks through those lines. Align's Homebuying Budget Review starts from the monthly housing cost that feels comfortable and works backward, the reverse of the ratio math here.

## Questions about income and home prices in Michigan

### How much do you need to make to afford a $300,000 house in Michigan?

It depends on your rate, savings and debts, so no single salary answers it. The property tax alone, about $5,400 a year at Michigan's 36.0-mill median, uses about $19,300 of yearly gross income at a 28% housing ratio. Add insurance and the loan at your quoted rate, then multiply the monthly total by 12 and divide by 0.28 to find the income that fits.

### Can I afford to buy a house if I make $40,000 a year?

Possibly. At a 28% housing ratio, $40,000 of gross income allows about $933 a month for all housing costs, and about $1,200 a month for housing plus other debts at 36%. Programs such as FHA loans and MSHDA's MI Home Loan can help with cash to close. Start with the address's property tax, then test the rest in a calculator at your quote.

### What salary do you need for a $400,000 mortgage?

It depends on the rate, so a salary figure without one is a guess. Start with the tax, which follows the price, not the loan: every $100,000 of price adds about $1,800 a year at 36.0 mills, which uses about $6,400 of yearly income at 28%. Then enter the loan amount and your quoted rate in an affordability calculator to see the full income figure.

### How much house can I afford at $1,400 a month?

That depends on what the $1,400 has to cover: property tax, insurance and the loan together. Subtract the address's tax first. At 36.0 mills, a $250,000 home carries about $375 a month. Subtract an insurance quote, and what remains is what the loan can use at your rate. At a 28% ratio, $1,400 a month matches about $60,000 of gross income.

### Do lenders use gross or take-home pay?

Gross pay. A debt-to-income ratio divides your monthly debts by your gross monthly income, which is income before taxes and other deductions. Take-home pay still matters, because it is what your own budget runs on. That is one reason the amount you qualify for can feel larger than the amount you are comfortable paying.

### Does the seller's property tax bill tell me what I will pay in Michigan?

No. The seller's bill reflects a taxable value that was capped while they owned the home. The year after a home sells, its taxable value resets to the state equalized value, half of the assessor's market value, not automatically half the price you paid. In 2026 that reset raised a typical home's taxable value about 50.5%, per Align's uncapping study.

### What is the median income of Michigan homebuyers?

$91,000 in 2025, counting buyers with first-lien purchase loans on owner-occupied Michigan homes, per Align's Michigan Mortgage Market Report, built from federal HMDA data. The median home value on those loans was $285,000. Among the 10 counties with the most loans, median buyer income ranged from $80,000 in Genesee to $136,000 in Washtenaw.
